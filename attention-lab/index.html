The Attention Lab

You’ve probably been told phones are wrecking your attention. Test it for yourself.

Three experiments

  1. Two blocks of 24 trials. The first uses one rule, the second switches between two without warning. The gap between them is your switch cost, in milliseconds.

  2. A 300 word passage with fake notifications sliding in while you read. Open them, dismiss them or ignore them. Four questions afterwards.

  3. A bar fills for a minute towards something worth reading. Skip whenever you like. Skipping is recorded but nothing is held back.

  • About 6 minutes
  • No signup

Reading 300 words

The record on the side of the spacecraft

In 1977, NASA launched two spacecraft called Voyager 1 and Voyager 2. Their job was to photograph the outer planets and then keep going. Nobody expected them back.

Bolted to the side of each one is a gold plated copper disc about the size of a dinner plate. It is a phonograph record, the kind you play with a needle, chosen because a needle and a groove will still work long after every computer format has been forgotten. A committee led by the astronomer Carl Sagan decided what went on it.

The record carries greetings in 55 languages, from ancient Akkadian to modern Mandarin. It carries 115 images encoded as sound, including a photograph of a person eating and a diagram of human anatomy. It carries thunder, a heartbeat, whale song, and a mother speaking to her child.

Then there is the music, about 90 minutes of it, and the choices are stranger than you might expect. Bach sits next to a Navajo night chant. Javanese gamelan sits next to Chuck Berry playing Johnny B. Goode. The committee argued for weeks about that last one, because some members thought rock and roll was too juvenile to represent an entire planet.

Etched into the cover are instructions for playing the disc, drawn as pictures, since there is no written language you could safely assume a finder would know. A small patch of uranium 238 sits beside them and acts as a clock. Anyone who picks it up can measure how much has decayed and work out how long the record has been travelling.

Voyager 1 is now beyond the edge of the solar system. In roughly 40,000 years it will pass within about 1.7 light years of a faint star in the constellation Camelopardalis. The disc should still be playable in a billion years.

The result

The door study

In 1998, two researchers ran an experiment on a university campus. Someone holding a map stopped a stranger to ask for directions. While the stranger was talking, two workers carrying a large door walked between them.

Behind the door, the person with the map swapped places with a completely different person. Different height, different clothes, different voice.

Fewer than half of the strangers noticed.

It is called change blindness. Your attention is not a camera recording whatever is in front of you. It is closer to a narrow beam that lights up the thing you are working on and leaves the rest dark.

That is not a flaw. A brain that recorded everything equally would be useless, because you would never be able to find anything in it again. Which means the question was never whether your attention has limits. It has limits. The question is what you point it at.

Simons, D. J. and Levin, D. T. (1998). Failure to detect changes to people during a real world interaction.

Before you read anything into it

  • This is not a diagnosis. It cannot be. Six minutes on a web page is a demonstration, not an assessment, and nothing here can tell you whether something is wrong with you.
  • Everyone shows a switch cost. Adults show it, pilots show it, the researchers who study it show it. It is a normal feature of how brains work, not damage.
  • Block A always runs before block B, so part of the gap could be tiredness or practice rather than switching. That is a real weakness in the design and worth arguing about in class.
  • The only comparison worth anything is against yourself later, in similar conditions. Comparing your number to a friend's number mostly measures who was holding their phone more steadily.

Run this again in two weeks. Same time of day, same device, somewhere equally quiet. If the numbers move, that is the only result here you can actually learn something from.

What now

Three things to try

All three take an ordinary week. None of them ask you to give up your phone.

Count before you change anything

For one full day, mark a tally on paper every time you pick up your phone without deciding to. Use paper, not an app. Most people are wrong about their own number by a factor of three, and you cannot evaluate a claim about your attention without knowing yours.

Run one Pomodoro

Twenty five minutes on a single task, then a five minute break. Use a kitchen timer or anything that is not your phone. The break should be something you enjoy that does not involve learning anything new.

Bring this question to class

Everyone in the room has a switch cost. So what does the popcorn brain claim actually predict that a normal brain would not? If nobody can answer that, the claim might be describing something real and still be useless.
